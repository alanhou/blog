**Paper**: [2611.31228](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.31228) — Language Models Steer on Injected Uncertainty Tokens, but Fail to Self-Monitor In Situ\
**Author**: Daniel Theron\
**Category**: cs.AI\
**Reading scope**: Original arXiv v1 HTML, all nine main sections, four appendices and disclosures. Both figure PNGs were visually inspected; table values were read from HTML cells. The code and experiments were not independently audited or reproduced.

## The Problem

Consider a teaching example, rather than a recorded benchmark attempt. Three bags hold four apples each, and two apples are eaten. An assistant writes that the bags contain twelve apples, then writes **12 minus 2 equals 8**. It appends “95% confident” and proceeds from the wrong number.

A system that sends the assistant back only when its confidence falls has a problem: the arithmetic failed, but its warning signal stayed high. Asking it to reconsider might still help. Those are two different questions—whether it notices the mistake, and whether it responds when someone supplies doubt.

Earlier work on reasoning search and self-correction uses intermediate judgments to decide where to continue or backtrack. Theron tests whether confidence numbers generated during the same reasoning pass can supply those judgments, then separately tests what externally inserted doubt does (§§1–2).

## The Explanation

### Watch the reported confidence at an actual error

After each written reasoning step, the models report two percentages: confidence that **this step** is correct, and confidence that **all steps so far** are correct. The second field is called “On-track.” In the apple example, a sensible whole-path assessment would have to account for the bad subtraction, even if a later step consistently uses its result (§3.1; Appendix A).

The study uses Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct, Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct, Mistral-7B-Instruct-v0.3 and Phi-4, which has 14.7 billion parameters. They generate eight attempts per question on arithmetic word problems from GSM8K and a selected intermediate-difficulty subset of MATH-500. Qwen and Llama receive 550 questions each; Mistral and Phi-4 receive 400 (§3.2).

The **15,200 attempts** are the starting pool. Formatting checks and selection of questions with mixed success produce 3,873 traces. The selection requires at least four correctly formatted attempts and a solve rate between 15% and 85% among those attempts. After 35 inconsistent label cases are excluded, the analysis contains **2,017 traces with a judged reasoning error** and 1,821 clean traces. These counts describe a selected set where models sometimes succeed and sometimes fail; they do not estimate error prevalence across all attempts (§3.3; Appendix C).

An automated judge, Claude Opus 5.5, receives reference solutions and numbered reasoning steps with the confidence tags removed. It marks the first invalid step. The model's confidence can then be compared with an error location determined without seeing that confidence (§3.3).

At that first error, mean On-track ratings remain **95.1%, 97.2%, 99.2% and 99.9%**, respectively, for Qwen, Llama, Mistral and Phi-4. These are numbers the models emit, rather than measured probabilities of being right. For errors with a preceding step, a drop of at least ten percentage points occurs in only **8.7%** of Llama traces and at most **1.6%** in each of the other three models (Table 2; Figure 1).

Could smaller changes still help identify bad reasoning? The paper compares good and off-track steps at the same step index. Its ranking measure, AUROC, is 0.5 for chance discrimination; the On-track results range from **0.500 to 0.561**. This is weak discrimination, rather than literally no signal in every model. Comparing at the same position matters because later steps are more likely to follow an earlier error: step number alone can look predictive without detecting a mistake (§4.2; Table 2).

The study also tests a proposed “confidence ratchet”: perhaps confidence briefly drops at an error and then rebounds unusually fast, hiding the mistake. It finds no supporting rebound effect among 117 observed dips, most of them from Llama. The main observed problem is that a substantial dip rarely appears in the first place. This null result does not establish why the monitoring fails (§5).

### Supply doubt while keeping the reasoning prefix fixed

Now freeze the apple solution immediately after its bad subtraction. Keep that reasoning text and change the two confidence fields together. One continuation sees **95% / 95%**, another **50% / 50%**, and another **10% / 10%**. The low values supply a warning without explaining the arithmetic error or giving the right answer.

That is the paper's intervention design. Actual prefixes end at a judge-localized first error that is neither the first nor the final step. Other controls preserve the original ratings or append an explicit “Wait, I think Step … contains an error” instruction. Because both numerical fields change together, the experiment measures their joint effect; it does not identify which field does the steering (§6.1; Appendix B).

Each condition is tested with ordinary formatting instructions and with an additional rule: **if On-track is below 50%, re-examine previous steps and fix an error before continuing**. The paper calls these the passive and active arms. The error location is supplied by the study's judge, so this is a test of recovery after targeted feedback, rather than a working autonomous error detector.

For Qwen, Llama and Mistral, the study uses 80 error prefixes per model and eight continuations per condition: 640 continuations in each cell. Phi-4 has 25 error prefixes and 200 continuations per cell. Of **27,568 continuations**, 21,200 start from error prefixes and 6,368 from clean prefixes. Repeated continuations are grouped by problem item in the statistical analysis (§6.1).

With ordinary instructions, replacing the format-matched high ratings with low ones increases correct final answers in all four models:

| Model | High ratings: 95% | Low ratings: 10% |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| Qwen2.5-7B | 21.2% | 27.5% |
| Llama-3.1-8B | 10.6% | 20.8% |
| Mistral-7B | 4.7% | 8.4% |
| Phi-4 | 2.0% | 24.5% |

The matched format helps distinguish a response to the doubt cue from a generic effect of adding text. All four passive comparisons are statistically significant in the paper's item-clustered analysis. Phi-4's estimates have wide intervals because they come from a small problem set: its 24.5% low-rating result has a reported 95% interval of **12.5% to 38.0%**. The outcome grades the **final answer**, which does not certify every revised reasoning step (§6.2; Table 3; Figure 2).

The explicit recheck rule strengthens recovery in some settings. For Llama, low ratings raise correct answers from **16.6% to 32.5%** relative to high ratings in the active arm. Yet **92.3%** of its low-rating continuations contain backtracking language. Words indicating reconsideration are counted through a regex detector; they are a behavior measure, not successful repair. Under the active low-rating condition, all four models still answer correctly less than 40% of the time (Tables 3–4; Appendix D).

### Check what the same warning does to sound reasoning

In the apple example, suppose the assistant correctly wrote that twelve minus two equals ten. A false warning now sends it back over a step that was already sound.

The paper tests that cost on clean prefixes matched to error-prefix depths. A clean prefix does not guarantee a correct resampled continuation: the comparison asks how often the final answer remains right under high versus low ratings. For Llama in the active arm, retention falls from **76.2% to 53.4%**, a **22.8-percentage-point loss**, across 63 clean prefixes with eight continuations per cell (Table 5; Figure 2).

An external monitor therefore needs more than the ability to trigger reconsideration. Among its flagged steps, enough must actually contain errors to outweigh damage from false alarms. Combining Llama's active error-recovery gain of about 15.9 points with its clean-prefix loss gives the paper's **58.9% break-even precision** estimate: roughly 59 out of 100 flagged steps would need to be real errors under this single-intervention comparison (§6.3; Equation 3).

That estimate uses the high-rating baseline and the study's selected prefixes. It is a model of one intervention, not an end-to-end guarantee for a monitor that checks every step. Its rates and costs need to be measured again for the actual workflow.

## Core Concepts

**A reported number and an error signal.** The “95%” after the apple subtraction is generated text. A calibrated 95% would correspond to about 95 correct cases out of 100 comparable predictions. Useful error ranking asks a different question: does the score separate mistakes from sound steps well enough to choose where to intervene?

**A fixed prefix.** Keeping the bad subtraction unchanged while varying its appended ratings lets the study test whether the cue changes downstream behavior. It does not demonstrate that the model would have produced a useful warning on its own.

**Precision among warnings.** If a monitor flags the correct subtraction as suspect, its intervention can waste or spoil useful work. Precision asks what fraction of its warnings are true errors; recall asks what fraction of actual errors it catches. The reported break-even estimate concerns the first quantity and the measured recovery-versus-damage trade-off.

## The Insight

The useful distinction is between finding a place to reconsider and successfully reconsidering it. In these models and this format, externally supplied doubt can influence continuation while self-generated confidence provides little help locating errors. A system should evaluate both functions before connecting them into a backtracking loop.

The clean-prefix experiment adds a further condition: a responsive model can follow a bad warning too. Stronger responsiveness does not by itself make the combined system more reliable.

## A Critical Reading

The study's paired intervention makes a specific behavioral claim credible: changing numerical doubt at a known error changes the chance of a correct continuation. The numerical control, unchanged reasoning prefix and clean-prefix comparison each answer a different part of that claim. The study remains about selected math questions, four dense instruction-tuned models and fixed prompts. It does not establish the same failure in large or native reasoning models (§§3, 6, 8).

Error localization is useful but imperfect. On ProcessBench, the judge identifies the exact first error in **85 of 100 error traces**, and a location within one step in 94. The paper's author checks 30 experimental traces from Qwen and Llama, agreeing exactly in 28 cases. These checks and the flat confidence around neighboring steps support the reading, but the small author audit does not validate every model or trace (Table 1; §8; Appendix C).

The paper leaves the internal explanation open. A separate evaluation pass on an existing derivation and a comparison with token probabilities or predictive entropy would help distinguish difficulty evaluating errors from difficulty reporting uncertainty while generating. Those controls were not performed. The results therefore do not show that the models secretly recognized the errors, or that generating text necessarily prevents recognition (§§7.1–8).

The intervention also changes both ratings together and acts at one specially selected point. Its threshold instruction is not a reliable numerical switch: Qwen frequently rechecks at exactly 50%, despite the instruction saying “below 50%.” Repeated false alarms, missed errors, delayed interventions and extra inference cost require a fuller deployment evaluation; the one-step precision estimates do not settle those questions (§6.3; §8; Appendix A.2).

## What You Can Use

For the apple assistant, evaluate whether a warning catches the subtraction error and whether acting on it improves the answer. Keep a parallel set of correct subtractions to measure what the same intervention damages. Counting more “wait” or “recheck” language would miss the distinction the paper exposes.

For a new reasoning workflow, this suggests testing the monitor and recovery policy separately, then together. A calculator or another task-specific check could supply grounded feedback where available; that is a design implication, rather than an evaluated comparison in this paper. Estimate the trade-off against the workflow's real no-intervention baseline before adopting a trigger threshold.

The practical lesson is to measure the usefulness of a warning at its source and at its consequences. A confidence field earns a role in control only through that evaluation.

## 问题

先用一个教学例子，不是论文里的实测记录：三袋苹果，每袋四个，吃掉两个。
助手先算出一共有十二个，下一步却写成了**十二减二等于八**。
它在这步后面标上“有 95% 的把握”，然后沿着错数继续算。

如果系统只在把握下降时让助手回头，这次算错就不会触发提醒。
但此时让它重新想一遍，也许又能改对。
这里有两件事：它能否发现错误，以及有人提醒之后，它能否作出有用的回应。

推理搜索和自我纠错的已有研究，会用中途判断决定继续、回看或舍弃一条路。
Daniel Theron 把这两个环节拆开：先检查模型边写边报的把握能否识别错误，再看外部放进去的怀疑怎样改变后续推导（§§1–2）。

## 翻译

### 先看算错时，把握有没有动

实验要求模型每写完一步，就报两个百分数：**这一步对不对**，以及**到目前为止是否全都对**。
第二项叫 On-track，可以理解为“整条推导还走在正确的路上吗”。
苹果例子里，后面哪怕很认真地沿用“八个”，整条推导也已经包含那次错误减法（§3.1；附录 A）。

参与实验的是 Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct、Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct、Mistral-7B-Instruct-v0.3 和有 147 亿参数的 Phi-4。
题目来自 GSM8K 的算术应用题，以及 MATH-500 中筛出的中等难度题。
每题生成八次；Qwen、Llama 各做 550 题，Mistral、Phi-4 各做 400 题（§3.2）。

**15,200 次作答**只是最初的池子。
先筛格式，再挑有时答对、有时答错的题：至少留下四份格式合格的作答，其中答对比例在 15% 到 85% 之间。
这得到 3,873 条推导；再排除 35 条判定不一致的情况，分析集包含**2,017 条有步骤错误的推导**和 1,821 条正确推导。
这些数是在特选题目里的统计，不能拿来算全部作答的出错比例（§3.3；附录 C）。

负责找出第一处错误的是 Claude Opus 5.5。
它能看参考解法和带编号的推导，但模型自己报的把握已经删掉。
这样就能把报出的把握与另一项判定给出的错误位置对照，避免判断者受到这些数字影响（§3.3）。

到了第一处错误，四个模型报出的整条推导把握，均值仍分别为 **95.1%、97.2%、99.2%、99.9%**。
这是模型写出来的数字，不是实际测得的正确概率。
对有前一步可供比较的错误，整条把握下降至少十个百分点的比例，Llama 也只有 **8.7%**，其余三个最多 **1.6%**（表 2；图 1）。

幅度很小的变化，会不会仍能分出好坏？
论文把同一个步骤位置上的正确与出错后推导拿来比较。
用来衡量排序区分能力的 AUROC，随机区分时是 0.5；四个模型的整条把握结果落在 **0.500 到 0.561**。
这说明区分能力很弱，不能说每个模型都毫无信号。
固定步骤位置很重要：越往后，越容易已经经过一次错误，只看“第几步”也可能显得有预测力，却没有真正识别哪步算错（§4.2；表 2）。

作者还检验了另一种猜想：模型或许先察觉不对，把握短暂下降，接着又异常迅速地恢复高把握，把错误盖住。
论文称它为“信心棘轮”。
在观察到的 117 次下降中，多数来自 Llama，没有找到支持这种异常回升的证据。
眼前更明显的问题，是大幅下降原本就很少出现；这个未见效应的结果，并没有查明自我监测失灵的原因（§5）。

### 推导先不动，换掉它后面的把握

回到苹果例子，把推导停在那次错误减法之后。
前面的文字保持原样，只把两个把握一起换掉：一份后续看到 **95% / 95%**，一份看到 **50% / 50%**，另一份看到 **10% / 10%**。
低值表达“这里没把握”，却没告诉助手哪里减错，也没给它正确答案。

这就是论文的干预办法。
实际截取的位置，是判断者标出的第一处错误，而且不能是第一步或最后一步。
对照还包括保留原始把握，以及加一句“等等，我觉得第……步有错误”。
两个百分数一起变，所以测到的是共同作用，不能据此分出到底哪个字段在驱动回看（§6.1；附录 B）。

每种条件又分两组提示：一组只保留普通格式要求，另一组追加规则——**如果整条推导的把握低于 50%，先回看前面的步骤，修正错误，再继续**。
论文分别称为被动组与主动组。
错误位置来自实验里的判断者，因此这里检验的是有针对性的提醒之后能否改进，还不是一套会自主找错的系统。

前三个模型各选 80 段含错推导，每种条件续写八次，也就是每个实验格 640 次。
Phi-4 只有 25 段，每格 200 次。
总共 **27,568 次续写**中，21,200 次从含错推导开始，6,368 次从正确推导开始。
统计分析按题目归组，处理同一题反复续写带来的相关性（§6.1）。

只用普通提示，两个低值就让四个模型的最终答对率都提高了：

| 模型 | 两项把握都是 95% | 两项把握都是 10% |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| Qwen2.5-7B | 21.2% | 27.5% |
| Llama-3.1-8B | 10.6% | 20.8% |
| Mistral-7B | 4.7% | 8.4% |
| Phi-4 | 2.0% | 24.5% |

高低值用同样的文本格式，帮助区分“怀疑起了作用”与“随便多塞一些文字就改变了输出”。
按论文以题目为单位的统计分析，四项被动组比较都达到统计显著。
Phi-4 的题目少，估计区间也很宽：低值条件下 24.5% 的答对率，论文报告的 95% 区间为 **12.5% 到 38.0%**。
这里评分的是**最终答案**，并不保证改写后的每一步都正确（§6.2；表 3；图 2）。

加上回看规则，部分条件下能改得更多。
主动组里的 Llama，从高值条件的 **16.6%** 提高到低值条件的 **32.5%**。
可它在低值条件下，**92.3%** 的续写都出现了回看措辞。
这项行为指标靠正则表达式识别，说明它说了要重想，不能等同于成功改对。
四个模型在“低值加回看规则”这项条件下，最终答对率仍都不到 40%（表 3–4；附录 D）。

### 再把同样的怀疑放到正确推导上

假如苹果助手原本正确写出了“十二减二等于十”，错误提醒就会让它回头折腾一段已经正确的推导。

论文把正确推导截到与错误推导相同的深度，检查这种代价。
前面正确，不代表重新续写一定答对；要比较的是高值与低值条件下，最终答案有多少仍能保持正确。
主动组里的 Llama，在 63 段正确推导、每格八次续写的比较中，保住正确答案的比例从 **76.2% 降到 53.4%**，损失 **22.8 个百分点**（表 5；图 2）。

所以，监测器光能让模型回头还不够。
在它发出的警报里，真错误必须占到一定比例，才能补回误报造成的损失。
把 Llama 主动组对错误推导约 15.9 个百分点的改进，与正确推导上的损失放在一起，论文算出 **58.9% 的盈亏平衡精确率**。
按这项单次干预的比较来理解，就是每报一百次，约有五十九次需要确实报在错误上（§6.3；公式 3）。

这个估计使用的是高值对照和特选推导。
它描述一次干预，不保证一个每步都检查的系统整体获益。
实际工作流里的基线、警报对象和代价，还得重新测。

## 核心概念

**报出的数字与有用的错误信号。** 苹果减法后面的“95%”，首先是一段生成文字。
概率校准得准，意味着一百次条件相近、报出 95% 的判断，大约能对九十五次。
拿它找错又是另一件事：分数能否把错误与正确步骤分开，足以决定在哪里干预？

**固定的推导前缀。** 前面的错减法不改，只换后面的把握，就能比较提醒怎样改变续写。
这不代表模型原本就会自行报出有用的怀疑。

**警报中的真错比例。** 正确减法也可能挨一次错误提醒，进而损失原有成果。
精确率看所有警报里有多少是真错误；召回率看全部真错误里抓住了多少。
论文的盈亏平衡估计讨论的是前一项，以及改错收益与误报损失的权衡。

## 洞见

找到该回看的位置，与在那个位置回看出成果，需要分别检验。
这些模型在这套格式下，会响应外部怀疑；自己边算边报的把握，却很难找到错误。
系统要把它们接成纠错回路之前，得先测清两个环节。

正确推导的对照又加了一个条件：模型也会听从不该听的提醒。
回应更积极，本身并不能保证组合后的系统更可靠。

## 博导审稿

固定推导、同格式高值对照，以及正确推导上的干预，各自支持一个不同判断。
它们让“在已知错误处改变怀疑程度，会改变后续答对机会”这项具体行为结论比较扎实。
范围仍是特选数学题、四个稠密指令模型和固定提示，不能直接延伸到更大的模型或专门训练的推理模型（§§3、6、8）。

找错的判断者也不完美。
在 ProcessBench 的一百条含错推导中，它有 **85 条**准确找到第一处错误，有 94 条落在相邻一步以内。
论文作者又手查了 Qwen、Llama 的三十条实验推导，二十八条与判断者的精确位置一致。
这些检查，以及错误前后相邻步骤的高把握，支持论文的解读；但小规模作者检查并不能验证每个模型、每条推导（表 1；§8；附录 C）。

模型内部为什么会这样，论文仍没有定论。
把已有推导交给模型另开一轮判断，再与词元概率或预测分布的不确定程度比较，有助于区分“不会评估”与“边写边报时传不出信号”。
这些对照没有做。
所以不能断言模型心里其实知道错误，也不能说生成文字必然妨碍识错（§§7.1–8）。

干预把两个把握一起改，并且只在特选位置动一次。
提示中的阈值也不是可靠开关：Qwen 在正好 50% 时仍经常回看，尽管要求写的是“低于 50%”。
反复误报、漏掉错误、延迟提醒，以及多花的推理成本，还需要完整的部署评估；单步精确率估计没有回答这些问题（§6.3；§8；附录 A.2）。

## 启发

对苹果助手，先测提醒能否抓住那次错误减法，再测回应提醒能否改善最终答案。
同时留一组正确减法，看看同样的提醒会破坏多少。
只数“等等”“重新检查”多了几次，会错过论文揭出的区别。

做新的推理工作流时，可以先分别测试监测与修正办法，再把两者接起来测。
有条件时，用计算器或任务本身的检查提供有根据的反馈；这是从论文得到的设计启发，不是论文已经验证的比较结果。
采用触发阈值前，按真实的不干预基线重新估计收益和损失。

一个提醒值不值得用，要同时看它从哪里来，以及听从它以后发生了什么。
报出的把握要经过这样的检验，才适合进入控制流程。
